# Idea forensics — collect, dump, report, interrogate

Sean's method, in his words: *list down the unordered information in bullets,
then work on it like a journalist and detective.* Ideas die when they are
invented in a vacuum; the good ones are usually already lying around in five
places, unassembled. Never start by thinking. Start by collecting.

## 1. Sweep every source before forming any opinion

Go wide before deep. The point is recall, not precision — a weak lead you
discard is cheaper than a strong one you never found.

- **Waku Memory** (MCP) — semantic, episodic and procedural. Usually the
  richest source, and the one most often skipped.
- **The repo** — `lab/` topics, `docs/`, commits, branches, `git grep`.
- **GitHub** — issues, PRs (including closed), commit messages, discussions.
- **Notion · Slack · Gmail** — where decisions get made and then forgotten.
- **Chat history** — this session, other sessions, screenshots the user pastes.
- **The web** — only for outside facts, never for what we already decided.

**Name every source you could not reach.** A disconnected MCP server or an
account you cannot see is a hole in the evidence, not a detail to skip past.
Say which one and what it probably held.

## 2. Dump unordered bullets

One fact per bullet. No ranking, no grouping, no synthesis. Attribute every
bullet to its source and date — `[WeChat, Sun]`, `[PR #230, Sep 27]`,
`[memory, 2026-09-23]`. Mark anything inferred or unverified as such.

Resist the urge to tidy. The mess is the raw material; tidying it early is how
you lose the one bullet that turns out to be the story.

## 3. Work it like a journalist

- Who are the characters, and what does each one actually want?
- What is the story — the one sentence a reader repeats to someone else?
- Where is the conflict or tension? A story without it is an announcement.
- What is the best quote already sitting in the evidence?
- What is the lede, and what is being buried?
- Who is the audience, and what do they get in the first thirty seconds?

## 4. Then work it like a detective

- Where do two sources contradict each other?
- What is assumed but never verified? Name the one fact that would settle it.
- The dog that didn't bark: what should exist and doesn't? Silence is evidence.
- What is the motive behind each ask — what is the other party really after?
- Which single unknown, if answered, collapses or confirms the whole plan?
- What is the cheapest test that would prove this wrong?

## 5. Tie it back to the product

Every idea that survives must name, concretely:

- **Waku Agent** — which file, pillar, arena or seam it touches or shows.
- **Waku Memory** — what it stores, retrieves or proves across agents.

An idea that cannot name both is a good idea for someone else's channel.

## Output shape

Bullets first, then the journalist's read, then the detective's open questions,
then one recommendation stated as a position worth arguing with. Keep the
unknowns visible to the end — a tidy conclusion that hides them is the failure
mode this skill exists to prevent.
